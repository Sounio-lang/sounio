# Three Temporalities in Psychiatry: A Framework for Time, Psychopharmacology, and High-Gain Temporal Systems

**Demetrios C. Agourakis**

Target: Frontiers in Psychology - Theoretical and Philosophical Psychology (Hypothesis & Theory)

Status: Draft, Section 1 revised

---

## Abstract

Psychiatry talks about time all the time, but rarely thinks about time. Episodes, longitudinal course, time to response, cumulative risk, and premature ageing appear scattered across protocols and narratives, without an explicit model that weaves together body, mind, and life history. This article proposes a conceptual framework in which three coupled temporalities --- biological time (tau_bio), psychological time (tau_psico), and narrative time (tau_self) --- are treated as dimensions of a single dynamical system, deformable by chronic mental states, environment, and psychopharmacology. The "temporal velocities" v_bio, v_psico, and v_self are defined as derivatives with respect to chronological time, allowing clinical phenomena such as accelerated ageing, subjective saturation of time, and collapse of future to be described as specific temporal configurations. Psychotropic drugs are reconceptualised as temporal operators that modulate these derivatives in class-specific ways. Building on a proposed temporal distortion framework for major antidepressant and antipsychotic classes (and GLP-1 receptor agonists, whose neuropsychiatric effects are increasingly documented), I argue that regimens considered effective at the symptom level may nevertheless produce temporal configurations that are poorly tolerated by specific individuals --- particularly those whose developmental profile is characterised by asynchronous coupling between temporal layers. These individuals, here termed high-gain temporal systems, are not distinguished by faster processing but by larger natural differentials between v_psico, v_bio, and v_self, amplifying the subjective impact of any deformation imposed by environment or medication. A clinical vignette synthesises the trajectory of a fictional high-gain subject under sedative regimens and metabolic interventions. Finally, I outline a research agenda involving operationalisation of the three temporalities, comparative studies, simple computational models, and implications for clinical practice. I predict that high-gain individuals will show greater discrepancy between chronological and psychological time estimates under sedative regimens than matched controls. The framework would be refuted if temporal velocity measures proved uncorrelated across the three layers, or if high-gain individuals showed no differential temporal response to pharmacotherapy. The aim is not to offer a new ontology of time, but a pragmatic lens for listening to, and managing, how different minds inhabit time --- and how psychiatry, by medicating them, alters that habitat.

## 1. Introduction --- why time matters in psychiatry

Psychiatry has always talked about time, but has almost never thought about time. We speak of longitudinal course, early or late onset, episode duration, time to response, and cumulative risk across the years. In parallel, we listen to patients who live "a future with no horizon", "endless days", "nights that do not pass", or who describe relapses as if they were "reliving the same decade on loop". Yet this temporal dimension remains treated as descriptive background, not as a structural part of our clinical and pharmacological models.

In practice, time appears fragmented into three poorly communicating registers. On one side, there is chronological time, the time of guidelines, schedules, and survival curves: dates, weeks of treatment, years of evolution. On another, there is biological time, implicit in expressions such as accelerated ageing, allostatic load, epigenetic clocks, and cardiovascular wear. Finally, there is lived time, which overflows in patients' narratives: minutes that drag under panic, weeks that become blurred under benzodiazepines, decades condensed into a single traumatic scene.

The central claim of this work is that these forms of time are not merely different perspectives on the same axis, but coupled layers of a mind-body dynamical system. A significant part of psychic suffering --- as well as many therapeutic and iatrogenic effects of pharmacotherapy --- can be read as specific deformations of these temporal layers and of the relations between them. This framework suggests that two patients on identical pharmacological regimens may inhabit radically different temporal landscapes, depending on their baseline temporal configuration.

### 1.1 Clinical examples of temporal distortion

In severe depression, the complaint "I have no future" is not just a figure of speech. Tomorrow ceases to appear as a space of possibility; the temporal horizon collapses violently. In mania, the opposite occurs: the present expands, the future is pre-consumed in grandiose plans, and the recent past loses relevance. In anxiety disorders, a few minutes in a queue or waiting room become long corridors of catastrophic anticipation. In dissociative states, entire intervals of chronological time vanish from conscious experience.

Against this backdrop, pharmacotherapy acts ambivalently. Benzodiazepines can shorten the subjective experience of suffering per hour lived, at the cost of reducing the sharpness of memory and narrative coherence --- what might be called a granulation of temporal texture. Stimulants compress chronological time into blocks of hyperfocus, narrowing the temporal window while intensifying what falls within it. Antipsychotics modulate the subject's relation to experiential continuity, sometimes stabilising it, sometimes flattening it; the distinction between typical agents (primarily D2 blockade) and atypical agents (5-HT2A/D2 modulation) likely maps onto different temporal signatures, though this remains unexplored. GLP-1 receptor agonists, increasingly documented for neuropsychiatric effects beyond metabolic targets (Tobaiqy & Elkout, 2024; Wilding et al., 2021; Kalia, 2025), reorganise the time of the body: weight trajectories, cardiovascular risk slopes, and metabolic ageing shift, altering how the patient projects their own life across years. The detailed temporal profiles of each class are developed in Section 3.

A key observation: a patient whose depression resolves under an SSRI may still report a collapsed temporal horizon --- the suffering is gone, but the future has not returned. Symptom remission and temporal restoration are not the same thing. This dissociation is invisible to current outcome measures.

### 1.2 The blind spot: when temporal layers are permanently misaligned

If the discussion above is already relevant for a typical patient, it becomes critical in a subgroup that is almost absent from psychiatric literature: individuals whose developmental trajectory is characterised by what the Columbus Group (1991) called "asynchronous development" and what Dabrowski (1964) described as heightened "overexcitabilities" --- intellectual, emotional, sensory, imaginational, and psychomotor.

These individuals, who meet criteria for profound giftedness in the developmental literature (Gross, 2004; IQ >= 145 or equivalent measures), are commonly described as processing "faster" or "more densely." But neither speed nor density captures the clinical phenomenon. What distinguishes their temporal experience is not a single elevated parameter but the *differential* between temporal layers:

- **Deeper recursion**: not more thoughts per second, but more levels of meta-observation. Thinking about thinking about thinking. The recursion stack is naturally deeper.
- **Wider temporal horizon**: not faster in the present moment, but a wider simultaneous window onto past and future. More counterfactual histories, more tentative futures, more parallel narratives held in awareness.
- **Higher coupling sensitivity**: a greater awareness of the drift between temporal layers --- between what the body can sustain, what the mind is doing, and what the narrative demands. This drift, which most people do not notice, is chronically salient.

Taken together, these features define what I will call a *high-gain temporal system*: not a system that runs faster, but one in which the natural differentials between v_psico, v_bio, and v_self are large. The mind generates possibilities that biology cannot yet support and that the environment may never accommodate. The suffering is not from being fast; it is from living in permanently misaligned time.

This asynchronous profile can be extraordinarily fertile in environments that offer sufficient challenge, language, and freedom for the subject to synchronise their internal time with the rhythm of meaningful projects. In restrictive, disorganised, or hostile environments, however, the same mechanism becomes a chronic source of suffering: the world responds too slowly, institutions operate in low resolution, available trajectories feel narrow. The subjective result is a particular form of temporal fatigue: the sense that body and context cannot keep pace with the time of the mind.

When psychotropic drugs designed for a typical temporal profile are introduced --- sedatives, mood modulators, metabolic agents --- we hypothesise that the temporal effects differ qualitatively from those in the general population. A benzodiazepine regimen that merely calms a typical patient may, in a high-gain system, collapse the recursion depth and narrow the temporal horizon, producing a subjective experience of cognitive amputation. A metabolic intervention that reorganises bodily time (weight, future risk) may remodel, far more deeply, the life narrative of a hyper-reflexive subject. These predictions are testable (Section 5) and, if disconfirmed, would weaken the framework.

It is important to note that many profoundly gifted individuals present with comorbid conditions --- ADHD, autism spectrum features, or specific learning disabilities (so-called twice-exceptional or 2e profiles; Webb et al., 2005). In these cases, the asynchrony between temporal layers may be compounded by additional desynchronisations: a v_psico that is high in some domains but low in others, or a v_self that oscillates rather than maintains a stable trajectory. The high-gain model should not be read as describing a homogeneous population, but as identifying a *temporal configuration* that amplifies the effects of any intervention.

### 1.3 Conceptual gap and aims of the article

Different literatures address fragments of this problem: studies on time perception in depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder; work on stress-related accelerated ageing; research on narrative, prospection, and continuity of self; reviews on psychotropic drugs and subjective experience; and the substantial body of chronobiology linking circadian disruption to mood episodes. What is missing is a model that unifies these threads and allows us to see:

1. How biological, psychological, and narrative time can be treated as coupled variables of a single system;
2. How chronic mental states deform temporal derivatives --- accelerating or decelerating different layers;
3. How psychotropic drugs can be reconceptualised as operators on temporal fields, rather than mere "symptom modulators";
4. And why asynchronous development, as seen in profound giftedness, should be understood as a high-gain temporal configuration --- a boundary condition that reveals the temporal structure of the model, in the same way that extreme temperatures reveal material properties invisible at room temperature.

The aim of this article is to propose a conceptual framework with clinical and theoretical use for time in psychiatry. I describe three temporalities --- biological, psychological, and narrative --- and introduce the idea of their velocities relative to chronological time. I then discuss how mental states and psychopharmacology act as operators deforming these curves, with the high-gain temporal profile as a boundary case. Finally, I outline clinical implications and a minimal empirical research agenda to test, refine, or refute the model.

The framework would be refuted if: (a) temporal velocity measures proved uncorrelated across the three layers (indicating independence rather than coupling); (b) high-gain individuals showed no differential temporal response to pharmacotherapy; or (c) the proposed drug-class temporal signatures failed to predict subjective temporal experience in controlled settings.

### 1.4 Positioning within existing work on temporality and psychopathology

The proposal to articulate biological, psychological, and narrative time in a single system does not arise in a vacuum. At least six strands of work have already explored, from different angles, the relation between temporality and psychopathology.

**Chronobiology and social zeitgeber theory.** Perhaps the most empirically grounded connection between time and psychiatry comes from chronobiology. Circadian rhythm disruption is implicated in bipolar disorder, major depression, and schizophrenia. The social zeitgeber theory (Ehlers, Frank, & Kupfer, 1988; Frank, 2005) proposes that life events disrupt social routines, which in turn destabilise circadian rhythms, precipitating mood episodes. Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT) builds directly on this model. Light therapy, melatonin-based interventions, and the circadian pharmacology of agomelatine further confirm that manipulating biological time has psychiatric consequences. This tradition provides the strongest empirical foundation for the biological time layer of our model, and we consider it central rather than peripheral. However, it focuses primarily on circadian and seasonal timescales, without systematically connecting to the subjective and narrative dimensions of temporality.

**Classical phenomenological psychopathology**, inspired by Husserl and Bergson, and developed by Minkowski, Straus, and more recently Thomas Fuchs (2013, 2018), offers detailed descriptions of alterations in "lived time" in melancholia, schizophrenia, and other conditions: melancholic depression as a slowing and emptying of the future, schizophrenia as fragmentation or disarticulation of temporal flow. This tradition provides a rich language for subjective and narrative time, but generally remains at a qualitative level and does not make systematic links to biological or pharmacological dimensions.

**Experimental work on time perception and interval timing** examines how individuals estimate, produce, or reproduce intervals of seconds or minutes, and how these processes are altered in depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder. Models of internal clocks (Matell & Meck, 2004) and striatal timing mechanisms offer a neurobiological basis for distortions of duration and passage of time. However, they typically focus on short windows (milliseconds to seconds) and task-specific performance, without explicitly connecting these distortions to life narratives or long-term trajectories.

**Predictive processing and active inference**, in which the brain is understood as a hierarchical prediction machine that minimises prediction error over time (Friston, 2010). Within this perspective, temporality is not just "duration" but a structural dimension of the hierarchies of prediction and update. Psychiatric disorders can be read as failures of precision, prioritisation, or updating of beliefs at different temporal scales. Some authors have begun to explore personal narratives as forms of active inference that stabilise expectations about self and world across time (Constant, Ramstead, Veissiere, & Friston, 2019; Bruineberg & Rietveld, 2014). Yet the mapping between these computational hierarchies and the biological, subjective, and narrative layers described in the clinic remains largely implicit.

**Prospection, mental time travel, and narrative identity** studies how individuals remember the past and simulate possible futures (Schacter, Addis, & Buckner, 2007). This literature has shown that depression, PTSD, and other conditions are associated with reduced specificity of autobiographical memory, impoverished future imagery, and collapse of the temporal horizon. These findings are very close to what we call narrative time, but they rarely engage with pharmacology or with explicit models of biological time.

**The time perspective literature**, developed by Zimbardo and colleagues (Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999, 2008), describes relatively stable profiles of orientation towards past, present, and future, linked to well-being and psychopathology. "Present-fatalist" profiles, for example, are associated with lower perceived control over time and life, while balanced future-oriented profiles correlate with better mental health. This approaches the narrative axis but does not systematically couple it to biological and subjective time nor to psychopharmacology.

**Interoception and embodied time.** The experience of time is deeply tied to bodily signals --- heartbeat, breathing, hunger cycles. Craig (2009) proposed that the insular cortex integrates interoceptive signals into a series of "emotional moments" that constitute the felt passage of time. Wittmann (2009) has shown that body awareness modulates time perception. This strand is critical for connecting psychological time to biological time and for understanding how metabolic interventions (including GLP-1 agonists) might alter temporal experience through bodily channels.

The framework proposed here engages with all six traditions, but aims to take two additional steps. First, by making explicit the idea of three coupled temporalities and their relative velocities, it brings phenomenological and narrative descriptions into contact with biological markers (allostatic load, epigenetic clocks, circadian phase) and measurable distortions in time perception. Second, by treating mental states and psychotropic drugs as temporal operators, it connects this system directly to everyday psychiatric practice and invites a different question: not only "which symptoms does this drug change?" but "which temporal configuration does it create --- and for whom?" In this context, the high-gain temporal profile is not introduced as an additional diagnosis but as a condition in which the same distortions in (v_bio, v_psico, v_self) have more intense phenomenological and clinical consequences, precisely because the inter-layer differentials are already large.

## 2. The Three Temporalities

This section defines the three temporal layers and introduces their formal representation as velocities relative to chronological time. The goal is not mathematical precision for its own sake, but a vocabulary that allows clinical observations to be stated with enough structure to be tested, compared, and eventually refuted.

### 2.1 Biological time (tau_bio)

Biological time refers to the pace at which the body ages, repairs, and entrains to environmental cycles. It is not a single variable but a family of processes operating at different scales, from milliseconds (heartbeat intervals) to decades (cumulative organ wear). Three subdomains are particularly relevant to psychiatry.

**Circadian and ultradian rhythms.** The most fundamental biological clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which organises approximately 24-hour cycles of sleep-wake, hormone secretion, body temperature, and metabolic activity. Disruption of these rhythms is implicated in bipolar disorder, major depression, and schizophrenia. The social zeitgeber theory (Ehlers, Frank, & Kupfer, 1988) proposes a causal chain: life events disrupt social routines, which destabilise circadian rhythms, which precipitate mood episodes. This theory has generated one of the few time-based interventions in psychiatry: Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT; Frank, 2005), which targets the regularity of daily routines as a way to stabilise biological time. Light therapy and agomelatine (a melatonin agonist / 5-HT2C antagonist) act directly on circadian phase. In our framework, circadian disruption is a specific deformation of v_bio: the velocity of biological time becomes erratic, losing its entrainment to the 24-hour cycle. The clinical consequence is that the body's internal day no longer corresponds to the social day --- a temporal misalignment that precedes and predicts mood instability.

**Allostatic load and accelerated ageing.** Chronic stress produces cumulative wear on physiological systems --- what McEwen (1998) called allostatic load. This wear can be measured through composite biomarker indices (cortisol dysregulation, inflammatory markers, metabolic syndrome components, cardiovascular parameters) and, more recently, through epigenetic clocks (Horvath, 2013), which estimate biological age from DNA methylation patterns. In psychiatric populations, biological age consistently exceeds chronological age: a 40-year-old with chronic depression may have the epigenetic profile of a 48-year-old. This acceleration of v_bio is not a metaphor; it is measurable, and it has consequences for morbidity and mortality that are independent of symptom severity.

**Interoceptive timing.** The felt passage of time is tied to bodily signals. Craig (2009) proposed that the anterior insula integrates interoceptive information --- heartbeat, breathing, hunger, visceral sensations --- into a series of "emotional moments" that constitute the subjective flow of time. Wittmann (2009) showed that individuals with greater body awareness report different temporal experiences than those with low interoceptive sensitivity. This strand connects biological time to psychological time: the body is not just a clock running in the background, but the medium through which time is felt. Metabolic interventions (including GLP-1 agonists, which alter appetite, satiety, and weight trajectories) may therefore change temporal experience not only through long-term biomarker shifts but through immediate changes in interoceptive signals.

In summary, v_bio is the rate at which the body ages and cycles relative to chronological time. It can be estimated through epigenetic age acceleration, circadian phase stability, allostatic load indices, and interoceptive sensitivity measures. When v_bio > 1 (relative to a population norm), the body is ageing faster than the calendar; when v_bio oscillates erratically, circadian entrainment has broken down.

### 2.2 Psychological time (tau_psico)

Psychological time refers to the subjective experience of duration, passage, and temporal structure. It encompasses how long a minute feels, how quickly a year seems to have passed, and how wide the temporal window of the present moment is.

**Interval timing.** The capacity to estimate, produce, and reproduce intervals of seconds to minutes has been studied extensively in psychiatric populations. The striatal beat-frequency model (Matell & Meck, 2004) proposes that interval timing depends on cortical oscillators whose phase is read by striatal medium spiny neurons, with dopaminergic modulation setting the "clock speed." In depression, time intervals are typically overestimated (a minute feels longer than it is); in mania, the opposite tendency is often observed. Anxious states dilate brief intervals through attentional mechanisms: when attention is directed towards time itself, duration expands. This laboratory-scale phenomenon maps onto clinical reports of "endless" panic attacks that last, by the clock, only minutes.

**Passage of time.** Distinct from interval estimation is the metacognitive judgment of how quickly time seems to pass. A patient may accurately estimate that ten minutes have elapsed, yet report that the morning "dragged interminably." Passage-of-time judgments are sensitive to affect (negative states slow passage), engagement (flow states accelerate it), and novelty (routine compresses retrospective duration). In our framework, v_psico captures this: a high v_psico means time passes quickly (the mind moves through experience at pace), while a low v_psico means time drags (each moment is prolonged).

**Temporal integration windows.** Perception is not continuous but structured into discrete "windows" of integration, typically on the order of 2--3 seconds for audiovisual binding. These windows may be altered in schizophrenia (fragmented, too narrow) and in states of flow or meditation (expanded). The width of the integration window determines the "grain" of temporal experience --- how much happens in each perceptual "now."

**The distinction between clock speed and felt passage.** A critical point for our framework: interval timing (clock-level estimation) and passage of time (metacognitive judgment) are dissociable. A patient on a benzodiazepine may have impaired interval timing (cannot accurately estimate 30 seconds) while reporting that time "flies" (high passage rate). Conversely, a depressed patient may estimate intervals accurately while reporting that time "stands still." v_psico is intended to capture the felt passage --- the experiential velocity --- rather than the clock mechanism alone. This is where the phenomenological tradition (Fuchs, 2013) and the experimental tradition converge: both describe alterations in how time is lived, but from different angles.

### 2.3 Narrative time (tau_self)

Narrative time refers to the temporal structure of the self --- the way an individual connects past, present, and future into a coherent life story. It operates on the scale of months, years, and decades, and it is the register in which identity, meaning, and purpose are constructed.

**Prospection and mental time travel.** Schacter, Addis, and Buckner (2007) showed that the same brain network (the default mode network) supports both episodic memory and future imagination. Remembering the past and imagining the future share a constructive process: both involve recombining stored elements into coherent scenarios. In depression, this process is impaired: autobiographical memories become overgeneral (lacking specific detail), and future images become impoverished or absent. The clinical complaint "I have no future" reflects a genuine deficit in the constructive machinery of narrative time, not merely a mood state.

**Temporal horizon.** The temporal horizon is the furthest point into the future that a person can meaningfully imagine themselves existing. In healthy adults, this extends years or decades: plans, goals, anticipated milestones structure the narrative arc. In severe depression, the horizon collapses to days or hours. In mania, it expands wildly but without the detailed construction that gives it weight. In suicidal states, the horizon may collapse entirely: the future becomes not merely empty but impossible. In our framework, v_self captures the rate at which the narrative unfolds and the reach of its horizon. A high v_self means the self is actively generating future possibilities, connecting past to present to future with fluency. A low v_self means the narrative has stalled: the past is disconnected, the future is blank, the present has no trajectory.

**Identity continuity.** Narrative time is also the register of identity: the sense of being the same person across time. Dissociative states fracture this continuity. PTSD collapses it by making the traumatic past perpetually present. Personality disorder can be understood partly as instability of narrative time --- a self that cannot maintain a coherent temporal trajectory. The time perspective literature (Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999) describes relatively stable orientational profiles (past-negative, present-fatalist, future-oriented) that shape how individuals inhabit narrative time. A "present-fatalist" profile, for instance, is associated with reduced agency and constricted narrative horizon.

### 2.4 Formalisation: temporal velocities and the state vector

Let t denote chronological time (clock time, calendar time). For each individual, at each moment, we define three temporal velocities:

- **v_bio(t)** = d(tau_bio)/dt --- the rate at which the body ages and cycles relative to chronological time. Estimated via epigenetic age acceleration, circadian phase coherence, allostatic load trajectory.

- **v_psico(t)** = d(tau_psico)/dt --- the rate at which subjective time passes. Estimated via passage-of-time judgments, temporal distortion scales, interval timing performance.

- **v_self(t)** = d(tau_self)/dt --- the rate at which the life narrative unfolds. Estimated via temporal horizon measures, autobiographical memory specificity, prospection fluency, time perspective profiles.

The temporal state of an individual at time t is the vector **V(t)** = (v_bio, v_psico, v_self). A "typical" individual in a neutral state would have V approximately equal to (1, 1, 1) --- biological, psychological, and narrative time all advancing roughly in step with the clock.

Clinical conditions correspond to specific deformations of this vector:

| Condition | v_bio | v_psico | v_self | Description |
|-----------|-------|---------|--------|-------------|
| Severe depression | > 1 (accelerated ageing) | < 1 (time drags) | ~ 0 (future collapsed) | The body ages fast, the mind slows, the narrative stalls |
| Mania | ~ 1 | > 1 (time flies) | >> 1 (grandiose futures) | Mind and narrative race ahead of the body |
| Anxiety | ~ 1 | << 1 (minutes feel like hours) | ~ 1 or < 1 | Time dilates in the moment, narrative may contract |
| Circadian disruption | erratic | variable | ~ 1 | Biological time loses phase coherence |
| Chronic PTSD | > 1 | variable | frozen (past = present) | Narrative time stuck; body wears |
| High-gain baseline | ~ 1 | > 1 | > 1 | Mind and narrative naturally faster than body |

### 2.5 Coupling, decoupling, and the asynchrony measure

The three velocities are not independent. They are coupled through physiological, cognitive, and experiential channels:

- **Bio-psico coupling**: Circadian disruption alters subjective time passage. Interoceptive signals (heartbeat, breathing) modulate felt duration. Inflammatory states associated with high allostatic load produce the "sickness behaviour" that slows psychological time.

- **Psico-self coupling**: How time feels in the moment shapes the narrative arc. If every day drags (low v_psico), the retrospective sense of the year is compressed --- paradoxically, time that felt slow in the moment feels like it vanished in retrospect. Conversely, rich and varied experience (high v_psico) expands retrospective duration. This is why travel makes years feel longer and routine makes them disappear.

- **Bio-self coupling**: Accelerated biological ageing changes the narrative of the life course. A 45-year-old with the body of a 55-year-old faces a different narrative calculus: fewer healthy years, altered risk trajectories, revised life plans. GLP-1 agonists that slow metabolic ageing may, by changing the body's trajectory, also change the story the patient tells about their future.

The key variable in our framework is not any single velocity but the **asynchrony** between them:

**A(t) = |v_psico(t) - v_bio(t)| + |v_self(t) - v_psico(t)| + |v_self(t) - v_bio(t)|**

When A is small, the three layers move in approximate synchrony: the body's rhythm, the mind's experience, and the narrative's arc are aligned. The individual inhabits a coherent temporal world.

When A is large, the layers are misaligned. The mind races while the body lags. The narrative demands a future the body cannot support. Or the body ages while the narrative remains frozen in the past. This asynchrony is, we propose, a core dimension of psychic suffering --- and a core target of intervention, whether pharmacological, psychotherapeutic, or environmental.

In the high-gain temporal profile (Section 4), A is naturally elevated: v_psico and v_self are high relative to v_bio, producing a chronic experience of temporal misalignment that is not pathological in itself but that makes the individual exquisitely sensitive to any further deformation. A drug that decreases v_psico (a sedative) may increase A rather than decrease it, if it slows the mind without slowing the narrative --- or, worse, if it slows the mind while accelerating biological ageing through metabolic side effects.

This is the central clinical insight of the framework: **effective treatment is not symptom reduction but temporal resynchronisation.** A regimen that resolves symptoms while increasing A produces what might be called a pharmacologically inhabitable but temporally uninhabitable state --- a life without suffering but also without temporal coherence.
